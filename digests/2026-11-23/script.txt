Hello and welcome to your weekly carbon capture, utilisation and storage digest. This edition covers the seven days from the sixteenth to the twenty-second of November, twenty twenty-six. It was a week dominated by policy and infrastructure: a power station consent in Scotland, a carbon market link between the European Union and the United Kingdom, a tense climate summit in Turkey, and a handful of engineering and funding milestones. We also have a research item on a new sorbent material, and a couple of lighter stories at the end. Let's get started.

We begin with capture projects, and a big one from Scotland. Scottish Ministers have granted planning consent, under what is called Section thirty-six, for the Peterhead Carbon Capture Power Station. This is a nine hundred and ten megawatt gas-fired plant developed jointly by SSE Thermal and Equinor, and it sits next to the existing Peterhead power station in the north-east of Scotland. The design aims to capture at least ninety percent of its flue gas emissions, which could mean up to one and a half million tonnes of carbon dioxide stored every year. Why does this matter? Because it is meant to be an anchor project for the Acorn carbon capture and storage initiative, which plans to reuse existing North Sea gas pipelines and could handle up to twenty million tonnes of carbon dioxide per year at its peak across the wider Scottish cluster. But there is an important caveat. The managing director of SSE Thermal said that moving toward a final investment decision depends on the commercial delivery of the wider transport and storage network. So consent is a milestone, not a finish line. Reports also note that the planned retirement of the old Peterhead plant has been pushed back, from twenty thirty to at least the middle of the twenty thirties, and environmental campaigners have reacted critically to the approval.

Our second story is policy, and it could matter a lot for storage economics in Europe. The European Union and the United Kingdom have concluded negotiations to link their emissions trading systems. The announcement came at the EU-UK Summit in Brussels on Friday. The arrangement would allow mutual recognition of allowances, and goods traded between the two markets would be exempt from each side's carbon border adjustment mechanism. Both sides aim to bring it into effect in twenty twenty-eight, subject to legal procedures, so there is still a way to go. For our field, the interesting part is storage. The Carbon Capture and Storage Association estimates that a connected storage market across Europe could save industrial emitters an average of about eleven euros per tonne, and companies in European Union member states could see offshore transport and storage costs fall by up to twenty-eight percent, roughly sixteen euros per tonne. The association also estimates that cross-border carbon dioxide transport and storage could bring the United Kingdom around four billion pounds a year in export revenue, and attract about seven billion pounds of investment by twenty forty. Those are industry-body estimates, so treat them as a view from advocates rather than settled fact. Separately, research by Frontier Economics suggests lower transaction costs alone could create around seven hundred and seventy million euros in shared benefits over the five years to twenty thirty.

Staying with policy, but moving to the global stage. At the COP thirty-one climate talks in Antalya, negotiators entered the final day divided over a revised draft cover decision, released on Thursday the nineteenth. According to Carbon Herald, the new text removes explicit references to phasing out fossil fuels, and instead elevates carbon capture, utilisation and storage as a central mitigation pillar. It introduces a provisional target to scale global carbon capture to at least five hundred million tonnes of carbon dioxide per year by twenty thirty-five, with a longer-term trajectory of one point three to two point five billion tonnes annually. For scale, current annual global energy-related emissions are roughly thirty-eight billion tonnes. So even the upper figure is a small fraction of today's emissions. The COP president defended the shift as vital for hard-to-abate industry. Opposition was sharp: Panama's climate minister said his country would block any consensus lacking a cap on oil production, and youth delegations protested inside the venue. Producers and some emerging economies, including India and Saudi Arabia, signalled support for including CCUS. Saudi officials mentioned plans to abate three hundred and thirty-five million tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent annually by twenty forty, and to build a nine million tonne per year storage hub. The summit was scheduled to close on Friday, and I could not confirm the final outcome, so that is one to watch.

Now to transport and storage. CO2next, the open-access liquid carbon dioxide terminal venture of Gasunie, Royal Vopak, Shell and TotalEnergies at the Maasvlakte in the Port of Rotterdam, has completed its front-end engineering design. The first phase has two marine jetties, each with three loading arms, plus four intermediate storage tanks and a pumping system. Those tanks act as a buffer: ships arrive intermittently, but offshore pipelines and reservoirs want a steady injection profile, so the terminal smooths the arrivals into a continuous pressurised stream feeding the Aramis offshore transport system, bound for depleted North Sea gas fields. Initial capacity is five point four million tonnes per year, scalable to ten million tonnes per year in later phases, and space is reserved for a third jetty and possible rail reception. The project now moves toward procurement tendering and a final investment decision. For anyone who models capture plants, this is the downstream reality: your product spec and delivery profile ultimately meet a shipping and buffering system like this one.

Next, funding and economics, with a note of caution. The Institute for Energy Economics and Financial Analysis published an updated analysis on the nineteenth of November on carbon capture in Alberta. Its conclusion is that Alberta's projects still have fragile commercial economics and depend heavily on public subsidies, shifting financial liabilities to taxpayers. Facilities there earn revenue mainly by selling emission performance credits. The analysis says revised carbon price schedules have lowered the revenue ceiling, while credit oversupply, weak compliance demand and cost inflation make cash flows less predictable. It warns that creating new credit classes or easing stringency could saturate the market further, raising the cost of price floors and government-backed carbon contracts for difference. The recommendation is that public support should be tied to verified, durable outcomes, such as measured emissions reductions, sustained capture performance and permanent storage, rather than upfront capital subsidies. It is an advocacy-think-tank view, but a useful reminder for techno-economic work: revenue assumptions matter as much as capture energy.

Our sixth story is a business deal with a solvent angle. Phelan Green, through its synthetic fuels arm Phelan eFuels, has awarded a contract to Honeywell to license its Advanced Solvent Carbon Capture technology and provide modular engineering for the first phase of an electro-sustainable aviation fuel complex at Saldanha Bay in South Africa. The announcement was made on the eighteenth of November and follows a final investment decision on Phase one in September. The plant would pair captured biogenic carbon dioxide with green hydrogen, and convert them via reverse water-gas shift and Fischer-Tropsch synthesis into synthetic aviation kerosene. Phase one is thirty-five thousand tonnes a year, with two more phases bringing total capacity to one hundred and forty thousand tonnes a year. The story shows solvent-based capture being packaged together with fuel synthesis under a single vendor, which is meant to cut integration risk and shorten construction schedules. The report does not give details of the solvent chemistry, so I will not speculate on that.

Now technology and research. Researchers at Oregon State University, with colleagues from UC Berkeley, the University of Oregon and the Aramco Research and Development Center, have developed a metal-organic framework called BVR-X. The work was published in Angewandte Chemie. The problem it tackles is a classic one for sorbents: industrial flue gas is humid, and water vapour tends to compete with carbon dioxide for adsorption sites. According to the researchers, BVR-X has an unusual internal structure that keeps water and carbon dioxide largely separated within its pores, so moisture does not block the sites needed for capture. In laboratory tests it captured carbon dioxide efficiently from a highly humid gas stream containing only four percent carbon dioxide, and it kept its performance through dozens of capture and release cycles. A patent application has been filed. This is laboratory-scale work, so there is a long road to industrial validation, but humidity tolerance is exactly the kind of property that decides whether a solid sorbent can compete with liquid solvents. I did not find a membrane or potassium carbonate item this week that I could verify, so this is our research highlight.

Moving to direct air capture. Japanese startup Planet Savers has secured over ten million dollars in support for its zeolite-based approach. That is made up of a pre-series A equity round of one point zero eight billion yen, about seven point two million dollars, led by ANRI, plus grant funding of more than one point six billion yen, about ten point seven million dollars, from the Japanese Ministry of the Environment and Tokyo Metropolitan Government programmes. In total the company has attracted about two point five billion yen since it launched in twenty twenty-three. Its system combines its own zeolite adsorbent, developed from research at the University of Tokyo, with an electrically driven capture system, and has been trialled in Japan, including at the Tokyo Bay waterfront and in agricultural settings. For removal, it is partnering with Australian company Carbon Vault on a storage-based project in South Australia.

Related to removal markets, Canada took a notable step. On the nineteenth of November, the government established a National Master Standing Offer for carbon dioxide removal credits, listing nineteen domestic projects as qualified suppliers from which federal departments can buy until the thirty-first of March twenty twenty-nine, with an option to extend. The technologies include direct air capture, bioenergy with carbon capture and storage, biochar, biomass storage and enhanced mineralisation. Among the names are Deep Sky, UNDO, Karbonetiq and the North Star project, being developed by Meadow Lake Tribal Council and Svante in Saskatchewan, one of only three bioenergy capture projects selected. Carbon Herald describes Canada as the first national government to establish competitive standing offers for permanent removal credits. For developers, reliable government demand can help unlock project financing.

Our tenth story is on utilisation. Germany's research ministry has committed more than twenty-four million euros to launch DISCO2, which stands for Disruptive Approaches to the Industrial Use of CO2. After a kick-off conference in Berlin on the tenth and eleventh of November, the three-year programme brings together forty-one partner organisations across thirteen research and development projects. The work spans capture mechanisms and catalytic, plasma-based and electrochemical routes to make products such as ethylene, acetate, methanol and synthesis gas. A coordination project manages knowledge transfer, standardised life cycle assessments will check that products really cut emissions compared with fossil equivalents, and a legal workstream will identify regulatory barriers. That life cycle piece is worth noting, since utilisation claims live or die on the carbon accounting.

And for something lighter, our final story. Mazda has said it will move its experimental onboard carbon capture system from motorsport to public road trials in twenty twenty-eight, aiming for commercialisation by twenty thirty-five. In the Fuji twenty-four hour race in June, an upgraded system with an exhaust dehumidifier, an adsorption and desorption unit and a storage cylinder captured eight hundred and four grams of carbon dioxide, close to ten times the eighty-four grams from the first track trials in November twenty twenty-five. The target is to capture between twenty and forty percent of exhaust carbon dioxide, and the prototype adds roughly fifty kilograms. Challenges remain, including offloading infrastructure and a supply chain for the captured gas. It is a niche application, but it shows the same adsorption and regeneration cycle that we model in plants, shrunk to fit a car.

Let's recap with some numbers. Peterhead: nine hundred and ten megawatts, up to one and a half million tonnes of carbon dioxide per year. The COP thirty-one draft: five hundred million tonnes per year by twenty thirty-five. CO2next: five point four million tonnes per year, growing to ten. Canada: nineteen carbon removal suppliers. The EU and UK link: around eleven euros per tonne average storage savings, according to industry estimates. And Mazda's eight hundred and four grams.
